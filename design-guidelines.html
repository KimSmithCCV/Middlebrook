Live & Build · Design Guidelines & Philosophy

Porches first. Garages second.

Great Western Crossing takes its cues from Des Moines’ most treasured historic neighborhoods, like Beaverdale, Waveland Park, Waterbury, and South of Grand. Every home is its own, and every home shares the same front porch, pitched roof, and welcoming front door.

Built at the human scale

The vision for Great Western Crossing centers on authentic architecture and a neighborhood built at the human scale, reinforced by Middlebrook’s agricultural amenities. Proper scale and proportion, combined with authentic details and variety along the street, create places to be treasured for generations.

Modern and traditional farmhouse styles fit the Agrihood’s character, alongside the craftsman bungalows and brick Tudors of historic Des Moines. Celebrating each home’s own details and textures lets modern, transitional, and traditional styles sit side by side.

Learn more about the thought and inspiration that went into designing the style for the Middlebrook Agrihood. Our architect, JP Curran, shares his ideas, drawings, and references that influenced the look and architectural design standards that connect the development to agriculture and make each neighborhood unique throughout the community.

Great Western Crossing Design Guidelines

Four styles, one street

Unless a lot calls for a specific style, choose one of four. Stay true to it: massing, materials, window grids, and roof pitches all follow the style’s long-established patterns.

Iowa farmhouses grew out of the national folk house. As the railroad spread in the late 1800s, lumber arrived cheaply from across the country, and builders moved from timber post and beam to light wood framing. Forms stayed simple: gable-front, gable-front and wing, I-house, and cross gable.

  1. Functional porches, often full or partial width, sometimes wrapping
  2. Simple pitched gable roofs, front or side facing, often cross gabled
  3. Simple square porch posts with little or no ornament
  4. Horizontal clapboard with corner boards; board and batten also works
  5. Simple eaves, open with exposed rafter tails or enclosed with a cornice

The early 20th century craftsman grew from the English Arts and Crafts movement, and the bungalow quickly spread across the country. Waveland Park, Drake, and Beaverdale all have a healthy stock of them. Most mix wood cladding and trim with brick or stone accents and foundations.

  1. Functional porches, full or partial width, on piers, columns, or both
  2. Low-pitched gable roofs, side or front facing
  3. Masonry piers or columns, often to the ground, sometimes tapered
  4. Deep open eaves with exposed rafter tails, knee braces, or brackets
  5. Exposed roof rafters

The Beaverdale brick draws on the Tudor revival style popular in the 1920s and 1930s, when many Beaverdale homes were built. Modeled on medieval English houses, the American versions used light wood framing and brick veneer, and were more modest than their English cousins.

  1. Steeply pitched roofs, usually side gabled, often with several gable fronts
  2. Large chimneys, often on the front facade
  3. Natural brick with stone accents; brick ends at inside corners
  4. Asymmetrical front facades
  5. Arched front entries and doors

A small share of lots can take a more modern direction, built on the forms and massing of the traditional styles. Details stay simple and concise, with a restrained palette of materials and color, fine proportions, and tailored detailing. Like the other styles, each home sticks to one set of patterns.

20% At most one in five lots on a block, approved first come, first served. A block is the length of street between two other streets or paths.

Room for the missing middle

A neighborhood is richer when it offers more than one size of home. Between the single-family house and the apartment sits what planners call missing middle housing: townhomes, cottages, and bungalow courts that open more price points and let people stay in the community as their needs change.

A bungalow court is a group of small, detached homes, one to one and a half stories tall, arranged around a shared courtyard instead of private backyards. Homes run about 600 to 700 square feet, or 1,000 to 1,200 for more bedrooms. They offer more privacy than an attached home, and the courtyard becomes a shared lawn, a place for a garden, or a spot to gather.

Middlebrook’s Cottages and Townhomes are part of this mix today, and future neighborhoods add more.

The front porch, an American icon

Every home has a front porch or a front entry courtyard. It is where neighbors stop to talk, and where the house meets the street.

How a home meets the street

Garages step back. A front-facing garage sits at least 5 feet behind the front of the house, with two bays at most. A third bay steps back 15 feet. Side-entry garages are encouraged, and some lots require them.

Driveways stay narrow. No more than 14 feet wide at the curb, widening only near the garage. A sidewalk connects the front walk to the porch.

Four sides, one house. Materials don’t change at an outside corner, trim is at least 4 inches wide, and window grids match across the house. Simple roof forms, never one of everything.

Trees on every lot. At least two shade trees, one in the front yard, plus one smaller ornamental tree. The street tree counts toward the two.

Working with the Town Architect

The Town Architect knows the guidelines best and reviews every single-family home in Great Western Crossing, together with the Architectural Review Board. The goal isn’t to slow a build down. It’s to make sure each home adds to a street that will be treasured for generations.

  1. First, schematic design

    Share the idea early

    Submit concept sketches while the design is still loose, along with any inspiration or precedent photos. A call or Zoom with the Town Architect can talk through questions and anything particular to your lot.

  2. Next, construction drawings

    Refine the plans

    Once the schematic design is approved, submit the final drawings. The Town Architect and the Architectural Review Board approve them or ask for changes to meet the guidelines.

  3. Last, final approvals

    Colors, lights, and landscape

    Exterior colors, exterior light fixtures, and the landscape plan are approved last. The smallest details, coordinated with the homes next door, are what make a street feel varied and whole.

Building in Great Western Crossing

Bring your own builder. Before plans are drawn, your builder and designer should talk with the Town Architect and the Architectural Review Board, who review every home.

See available lots
Step 1 Choose a lot

Start with the lot regulating plan (page 12 of the guidelines), and confirm your lot against the recorded plat. Most lots follow the standard requirements; some require a side-entry garage, an entry court, or a particular style.

Step 2 Review the lot and massing diagrams

Check the front, side, and rear setbacks for your lot, and place the porch within the build-to zone on pages 13 to 15. Corner lots face two streets and need extra care; a wrapping porch is encouraged.

Step 3 Choose an architectural style

Pick one of the four styles (pages 17 to 20), and design a modern but authentic take on it.

Step 4 Work out forms and patterns

Keep roof forms simple and true to the style. Masonry openings get real lintels and arches, dormer windows are trimmed and fitted, and horizontal transom windows are avoided on traditional styles.

Step 5 Plan the landscape

Coordinate street trees with the developer (page 34), plant evergreens along the foundation, and see the plant lists on pages 31 and 32 and fencing on page 33.
